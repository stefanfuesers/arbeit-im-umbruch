Why the future of work depends on strong communities

Hans Meves

The world of work is undergoing profound change. Digitalisation, artificial intelligence, demographic change, climate protection and global crises are not only changing the way we work. They also raise anew the question of the meaning, the use and the social significance of work.

While many discussions about the future of work are dominated by technological developments, another perspective is increasingly coming to the fore: work is not just an economic factor but part of a complex social, cultural and spatial fabric. It shapes communities, influences quality of life and largely determines whether communities are fit for the future.

The decisive question is therefore not only which technologies will change the world of work. The real challenge is how citizens, companies, municipalities, associations and educational institutions can jointly develop viable prospects for the future.

Work always takes place in social spaces

Every form of work is embedded in an environment. Companies are part of regional value chains. Employees live in towns and cities. Consumer decisions influence production processes. Educational institutions provide the skills for tomorrow’s world of work. Work, production and consumption therefore form a coherent socio-cultural system.

If a community is to remain economically successful and at the same time worth living in, these areas must be considered together. The future of work emerges where people cooperate, share knowledge and develop innovative solutions to social challenges.

Municipalities and regions in particular thus gain a new strategic significance. They become places where new forms of cooperation, value creation and sustainable economic activity can be tested and put into practice.

Knowledge as the foundation of a new work culture

The first prerequisite for future-proof work is knowledge.

This has long been about more than technical know-how or academic qualifications. Companies, associations, administrations and neighbourhoods hold an enormous wealth of experiential knowledge that often goes unused.

Many people have skills, competences and experience that hardly become visible in conventional organisational structures. At the same time, the amount of available information grows every day. The challenge therefore lies not in access to knowledge but in connecting and applying it meaningfully.

The world of work of the future therefore needs new forms of learning and knowledge exchange. Knowledge must be prepared in an understandable way, shared and developed further together. Only when individual knowledge becomes collective knowledge for action is the basis for lasting change created.

Innovation happens where people come together

The second prerequisite for a sustainable future of work is innovation.

Innovation is often equated exclusively with technical inventions or digital solutions. In fact, the most significant innovations frequently arise at the interfaces of different experiences, perspectives and competences. Innovation is therefore above all a social process.

New forms of work, regional cooperation models, sustainable services or innovative business models develop where people work on solutions together. Our communities have enormous potential for this, because they connect companies, educational institutions, administrations, associations and citizens in a shared space for living and doing business.

Rural municipalities and urban centres can turn this into a competitive advantage. Proximity between actors allows short distances, trusting relationships and a strong identification with regional development goals. What matters, however, is creating spaces for encounter and cooperation. Innovation needs places where ideas can emerge, be discussed and be developed further.

A new social understanding of work requires cultural change

The third prerequisite for tomorrow’s world of work is sustainability.

This is not only about environmental and climate protection. Sustainability means permanently combining economic performance, social responsibility and ecological compatibility. This requires a fundamental cultural change.

Production and consumption must be geared more strongly towards long-term value creation. Companies need new forms of responsible leadership. Employees are increasingly looking for meaningful activities. At the same time, customers expect sustainable products and services.

The future of work will thus be closely linked to the question of what contribution organisations and people make to the common good. Sustainability will no longer be an add-on topic. It is becoming a central success factor for companies, municipalities and regions.

The importance of regional spaces for the future

If knowledge, innovation and sustainability are the three supporting pillars of a future-proof world of work, the question arises of where they are put into practice. This is where regional (municipal, urban) spaces for the future are gaining importance.

These are open platforms for exchange, learning and joint development. Such spaces can be physical, digital or hybrid. Their aim is to bring together people and organisations who want to work towards a sustainable future for their region, their city or their municipality.

Key tasks of such spaces include:

  • promoting participation and co-creation,
  • connecting companies, municipalities and civil society,
  • developing regional innovation projects,
  • supporting sustainable business models,
  • exchanging knowledge and experience,
  • evaluating and further developing joint initiatives.

They create the conditions for ideas to turn into concrete change.

Rethinking work means shaping the future together

The future of work is not decided in boardrooms, ministries or technology centres. It is decided locally, in towns and municipalities, in companies, associations, educational institutions and communities.

Tomorrow’s world of work needs a new culture of togetherness. Knowledge must be shared, innovation developed jointly and sustainability understood as a task for society as a whole. The real change therefore lies less in new technologies than in new forms of cooperation.

Anyone who wants to understand work in upheaval must see work as part of a larger context: a system of knowledge, innovation, sustainability and regional development. Therein also lies a great opportunity.

If we succeed in connecting people, organisations and communities more closely, regions can become laboratories of a sustainable future. Then a world of work emerges that not only enables economic success but also fosters social cohesion, ecological responsibility and a high quality of life.

Work in upheaval therefore means one thing above all: not waiting for the future, but shaping it together.

An impulse contribution by Dr. Hans Meves, CIVICON | W3-impact

From the perspective of a community (in Ferdinand Tönnies’ sense of Gemeinschaft), this article attempts to carry the concept of New Work and the cultural change associated with it into an integrated approach of sustainability, socio-spatial development, participation and regional value creation. It is an attempt to discuss the importance of people and their communities as the actual bearers of a cultural change of work from the perspective of the NANK centres model (see graphic). Knowledge, innovation and sustainability are at the centre of the discussion as key levers.
