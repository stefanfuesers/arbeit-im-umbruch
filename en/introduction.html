Work in Upheaval

Hans Meves, Stefan Füsers

Work is changing faster than it has in generations. Artificial intelligence is taking over tasks that whole professions consisted of only yesterday. Industries are reorganising, and there is much to suggest that the greater part of this change still lies ahead of us.

Whether this change will make us freer is an open question. The sociologist Hartmut Rosa has described what has almost always happened when a new technology was meant to save time. Planes take us around the world faster, and since then we travel far more. An email is quicker to write than a letter, and since then we write far more of them. The time we gained flowed into new tasks, and the pace of life kept rising.

This is the challenge of artificial intelligence. We can finally use this technology in a way that gives people more freedom and takes pressure off them. That will not happen by itself. It requires change, in the life of each individual and in society.

We see this first and foremost as an opportunity. When machines take over part of the work, time can be freed up. What remains open is which work they take off our hands. Right now AI is writing poems and books, and we are still doing the laundry. The author Joanna Maciejewska has put into words a wish that many people share. AI should do her laundry and her dishes so that she has time for art and writing.

Behind this lies the question Frithjof Bergmann asked decades ago. What work do we really, really want to do? Only people can answer this question. We recognise whether work is meaningful by what it sets off in us and what it does for others. That is why people belong at the centre of this change.

We can only answer the question honestly if we are free enough to do so. Today many people shape their work around what earns money. Profit sets the pace, and little room is left for their own answer. A basic income takes away this pressure. People who do not have to fear for their livelihood can do what they really want to do.

Then work becomes something creative again. We can live out the joy of shaping things and turn to the question of who we could be and who we want to be.

Hartmut Rosa has developed a theory of our relationship to the world for this. At its core is resonance. It means the experience that the world answers when we turn towards it and contribute something. Work in which we feel this answer is work we experience as beautiful. Resonance and acceleration are closely linked. Anyone who is constantly running behind has hardly any time to feel what is going on inside them. In today's world this ability has largely been lost.

If we finally use new technology as a liberating technology, we gain an abundance of time. This time gives us the chance to start feeling again and to perceive ourselves. In this way we can develop our capacity for resonance anew. The change in work then becomes a change in our relationship to the world.

On this blog we accompany the transformation of work with impulses and ideas, joined by voices from science, art and practice. We look at what becomes possible.
